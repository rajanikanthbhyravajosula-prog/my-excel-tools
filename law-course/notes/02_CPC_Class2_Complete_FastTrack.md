# Class 2: CPC — The Complete Fast-Track
_Code of Civil Procedure, 1908  |  10 modules with cases and memory tricks  |  Law Refresher Course, Phase 1_

> **Before you cite a case in court**, check the citation on SCC Online, Manupatra or Indian Kanoon.

## Module 1: Foundation (Sections 1–9)
- **Sections vs Orders.** Sections are the law and only Parliament can amend them. Orders are procedure, and High Courts can amend them (S.122). Where they conflict, the Section wins.
- **Decree, Judgment, Order:** S.2(2), 2(9), 2(14).
- **S.9:** civil courts try every suit of a civil nature unless it is expressly or impliedly barred.

| Case | Point |
|---|---|
| *Dhulabhai v. State of M.P.* (1968) | Exclusion of civil court jurisdiction is not readily inferred. The civil court can still step in if a tribunal acted without jurisdiction or ignored natural justice. |
| *Kiran Singh v. Chaman Paswan* (1954) | A decree passed without **inherent** jurisdiction is a **nullity** and can be challenged even in execution. |

> **Trick:** "The civil court is the default doctor. A specialist (tribunal) takes over only if the law clearly says so."

## Module 2: Bars to a Suit (S.10, S.11, O.2 R.2)
### 1. Res Sub Judice (S.10): stay of the later suit
The **later** suit is **stayed** (not dismissed) when all of these apply:
1. Two suits are pending.
2. The matter in issue is **directly and substantially the same**.
3. The parties are the same, or litigate under the same title.
4. The earlier suit is in a competent court in India.

| Case | Point |
|---|---|
| *Aspi Jal v. Khushroo Rustom Dadyburjor* (2013, SC) | S.10 applies only when the **entire** subject matter is the same, so that the decision in the first suit would operate as res judicata in the second. |
| *Indian Bank v. Maharashtra State Co-op Marketing Federation* (1998, SC) | S.10 bars the **trial** of the later suit, not its **filing**. |

### 2. Res Judicata (S.11): bar on re-deciding a matter
**Essentials** (mnemonic: **"SAME-COMP-FINAL"**):
1. **Same** matter, directly and substantially in issue
2. **Same** parties, or those claiming under them
3. **Same** title
4. **Competent** court
5. Heard and **finally** decided

**Constructive res judicata (Explanation IV):** a point that *might and ought* to have been raised is treated as raised and decided.

| Case | Point |
|---|---|
| *Satyadhyan Ghosal v. Deorajin Debi* (1960) | Res judicata applies between **different stages of the same suit**. |
| *Daryao v. State of U.P.* (1961) | Res judicata applies to **writ petitions** too. |
| *Forward Construction Co. v. Prabhat Mandal* (1986) | Constructive res judicata applies to public interest litigation. |

| | S.10 Res Sub Judice | S.11 Res Judicata |
|---|---|---|
| Status of first matter | **Pending** | **Decided** |
| Effect | Later suit stayed | Later suit barred |
| Purpose | Avoid conflicting decisions | Finality of decisions |

> **Trick:** "S.10: wait, the case is still cooking. S.11: done, the case is already served."

### 3. Order II Rule 2: the whole claim at once
If a plaintiff leaves out part of a claim arising from the **same cause of action** without the court's leave, they cannot sue for it later.
- *Gurbux Singh v. Bhooralal* (1964)
- *Virgo Industries v. Venturetech* (2013): O.2 R.2 applies only where both claims arise from the **same cause of action**.

> **Hall example:** you sue a client for unpaid hall rent but not for the furniture damage. You cannot file a second suit for the furniture damage.

## Module 3: Where to Sue (S.15–21)
| Section | Rule |
|---|---|
| **S.15** | File in the court of the **lowest grade** competent to try the suit. |
| **S.16** | Suits about **immovable property** go where the property is situated. |
| **S.17** | If the property is spread across jurisdictions, sue in any of them. |
| **S.19** | For a wrong to a person or movables, sue where the wrong happened **or** where the defendant resides. |
| **S.20** | All other suits go where the defendant resides or works, or where the cause of action arose, **wholly or in part**. |
| **S.21** | Objections to territorial or pecuniary jurisdiction must be raised at the **first instance**. Otherwise they are waived, unless there was a failure of justice. |

| Case | Point |
|---|---|
| *ABC Laminart v. A.P. Agencies* (1989) | An exclusive jurisdiction clause is valid **only if** the chosen court already has jurisdiction. |
| *Swastik Gases v. Indian Oil* (2013) | Even without words like "only" or "exclusive", the intention to exclude other courts can be inferred. |
| *Patel Roadways v. Prasad Trading* (1991) | A company can be sued where its **subordinate office** is, **if** the cause of action arose there. |

> **Trick:** "Land: sue where the land is. Wrong: where it happened or where the defendant lives. Everything else: the defendant's home or where the trouble started."
> **Practical point:** put an exclusive jurisdiction clause in your hall booking agreement, e.g. "Courts at Nandyal alone shall have jurisdiction."

## Module 4: Parties and Pleadings (Orders I, VI, VII, VIII)
### Parties (Order I)
- **Necessary party:** no effective decree can be passed without them.
- **Proper party:** their presence helps the court decide completely.
- **O.1 R.10:** the court can add or strike out parties at any stage.
- **O.1 R.8:** a representative suit is one person suing or being sued on behalf of many with the same interest.
- *Mumbai International Airport v. Regency Convention Centre* (2010): the plaintiff is the **dominus litis**, the master of the suit, but the court can add necessary parties.

### Pleadings (Order VI)
- Plead **facts, not evidence or law** (O.6 R.2).
- **O.6 R.17, amendment:** amendments should be allowed liberally before trial. After trial begins, the party must show **due diligence**.

| Case | Point |
|---|---|
| *Revajeetu Builders v. Narayanaswamy* (2009) | Six factors for allowing an amendment. |
| *LIC v. Sanjeev Builders* (2022, SC) | **Latest** summary of amendment principles. Allow liberally unless the amendment causes injustice, changes the nature of the suit, or seeks to add a time-barred claim. |

### Plaint (Order VII)
**O.7 R.11, rejection of plaint** (mnemonic: **"C-U-S-B-D-N"**):
- (a) No **Cause** of action
- (b) **Undervalued**, and not corrected after the court's direction
- (c) Insufficient **Stamp** (court fee), not paid after the court's direction
- (d) **Barred** by law, e.g. by limitation
- (e) Not filed in **Duplicate**
- (f) **Non**-compliance with O.7 R.9 (copies and summons fees)

| Case | Point |
|---|---|
| *T. Arivandandam v. T.V. Satyapal* (1977) | A plaint that creates an **illusion** of a cause of action through clever drafting should be rejected at the threshold. |
| *Saleem Bhai v. State of Maharashtra* (2003) | Only the **plaint averments** are examined. The written statement is irrelevant at this stage. |
| *Dahiben v. Arvindbhai Kalyanji Bhanusali* (2020, SC) | Comprehensive restatement of O.7 R.11. A plaint that is plainly barred by limitation must be rejected. |

**Return of plaint (O.7 R.10):** when the court lacks jurisdiction, the plaint is **returned** to be filed in the proper court, not rejected.

### Written Statement (Order VIII)
| Type of suit | Time limit | Nature of the limit |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary suit | 30 days, extendable to **90** | **Directory** (*Kailash v. Nanhku*, 2005) |
| Commercial suit | **120 days** | **Mandatory**: no written statement after that (*SCG Contracts v. K.S. Chamankar*, 2019) |

- **Set-off (O.8 R.6)** is limited to an ascertained sum of money.
- **Counter-claim (O.8 R.6A)** can be any claim, and is treated as a plaint.

> **Trick:** "Ordinary suit: 90 days, the court may forgive. Commercial suit: 120 days, no forgiveness."

## Module 5: Trial Stage (Orders V, IX, X, XI, XIV–XX)
### Appearance and default (Order IX)
| Situation | Result |
|---|---|
| Neither party appears | Suit dismissed (R.3). Can be refiled or restored (R.4). |
| Plaintiff absent, defendant present | Suit dismissed (R.8). Restoration under **R.9** within **30 days**. |
| Defendant absent | **Ex parte** hearing (R.6). Ex parte decree set aside under **R.13** within **30 days**. |

To set aside an ex parte decree under **O.9 R.13**, show either that the summons was not duly served, or that there was **sufficient cause** for not appearing. *G.P. Srivastava v. R.K. Raizada* (2000): "sufficient cause" must be read **liberally**.

> **Trick:** "R.9 restores the Plaintiff's suit. R.13 rescues the Defendant from an ex parte decree."

### Other trial points
- **O.10:** the court examines the parties and refers the dispute to **ADR under S.89**. *Salem Advocates Bar Assn. v. UOI* (2005) upheld the amendments; *Afcons Infrastructure v. Cherian Varkey* (2010) explained which cases suit ADR.
- **O.11:** discovery and inspection. Commercial suits have a stricter disclosure regime.
- **O.14:** framing of issues. **Issues of law** such as jurisdiction or limitation may be decided first.
- **O.17 R.1:** a maximum of **3 adjournments** per party.
- **O.18 R.4:** chief examination is given by **affidavit**. Cross-examination happens in court or before a commissioner.
- **O.20 R.1:** judgment within **30 days** of the hearing ending, extendable to **60**.

## Module 6: Interim Reliefs (Orders XXXVIII–XL, S.94–95, S.148A)
### Temporary injunction (O.39 R.1 and R.2)
The **triple test** (mnemonic: **"P-B-I"**):
1. **P**rima facie case
2. **B**alance of convenience
3. **I**rreparable injury

| Case | Point |
|---|---|
| *Dalpat Kumar v. Prahlad Singh* (1992) | Established the triple test. |
| *Wander Ltd. v. Antox* (1990) | An appellate court should not interfere with the trial court's discretion unless it is arbitrary or perverse. |

- **O.39 R.3:** for an ex parte injunction, reasons must be recorded and notice served on the other side soon after.
- **O.39 R.3A:** the application must be disposed of within **30 days**.
- **O.39 R.2A:** breach of an injunction leads to attachment of property and up to **3 months** in civil prison.
- **O.38:** arrest or attachment **before judgment**, when the defendant is about to abscond or dispose of property.
- **O.40:** appointment of a **receiver**.
- **S.148A, Caveat:** entitles you to notice before any ex parte order. **Valid for 90 days.**

> **Trick:** "File a caveat before you sleep. The other side cannot get an ex parte order behind your back for 90 days."

## Module 7: Special Suits
| Provision | Topic | Key point |
|---|---|---|
| **S.80** | Suits against the **Government** | A **2-month** notice is required. Under S.80(2) a suit for urgent relief can be filed with the court's leave. |
| **O.37** | **Summary suits** on negotiable instruments and written contracts | The defendant must get **leave to defend**. *IDBI Trusteeship v. Hubtown* (2016) revised the leave-to-defend principles. |
| **O.32** | Minors | A next friend sues on the minor's behalf; a guardian ad litem defends. |
| **O.33** | Indigent persons | The suit can proceed without paying court fee. |
| **O.35** | Interpleader | Used when two people claim the same property from you. |
| **Commercial Courts Act, S.12A** | **Pre-institution mediation** | *Patil Automation v. Rakheja Engineers* (2022): mandatory unless urgent relief is sought; a suit filed without it is rejected. |

> **Trick:** for an O.37 summary suit, "No leave, no defence: the decree follows."

## Module 8: Death, Withdrawal and Compromise (Orders XXII, XXIII)
- **O.22:** legal representatives must be substituted within **90 days** (Limitation Act, Art.120), otherwise the suit **abates automatically**. An application to set aside the abatement must be filed within **60 days** after that (Art.121).
- **O.23 R.1:** the plaintiff can withdraw the suit. Without the court's permission, a fresh suit is **barred**.
- **O.23 R.3:** a **lawful compromise** must be recorded in writing and signed.
- **O.23 R.3A:** no separate suit lies to challenge a compromise decree. The challenge must be made in the same court.

## Module 9: Appeals, Reference, Review, Revision (S.96–115, Orders XLI–XLVII)
| Remedy | Provision | Where it lies | Time limit | Grounds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **First appeal** | S.96, O.41 | District Court or High Court | 30 days (District Court) or 90 days (High Court) | Facts **and** law |
| **Second appeal** | S.100 | High Court only | 90 days | Only a **substantial question of law** |
| **Appeal from orders** | S.104, O.43 | Appellate court | 30 or 90 days | Only orders listed in O.43 |
| **Review** | S.114, O.47 | **Same** court | 30 days | Error apparent on the face of the record, new evidence, or other sufficient reason |
| **Revision** | S.115 | High Court | — | Jurisdictional error only |
| **Reference** | S.113, O.46 | High Court | — | Doubt on the validity of a statute |

- **S.96(4):** no appeal in Small Cause suits up to ₹10,000, except on a question of law.
- **S.102:** no second appeal in money suits up to **₹25,000**.

| Case | Point |
|---|---|
| *Santosh Hazari v. Purushottam Tiwari* (2001) | The first appellate court must deal with the trial court's reasoning. It cannot simply affirm. |
| *Hero Vinoth v. Seshammal* (2006) | Explains what counts as a "substantial question of law". |
| *Kamlesh Verma v. Mayawati* (2013) | Review is **not** an appeal in disguise. |
| *Radhey Shyam v. Chhabi Nath* (2015) | Judicial orders of civil courts can be challenged only under **Art. 227**, not by certiorari under Art. 226. |

> **Trick:** Appeal = a higher court, a fresh look. Review = the same court corrects itself. Revision = the High Court checks jurisdiction only. Reference = the lower court asks the High Court for guidance.

## Module 10: Execution (S.36–74, Order XXI)
- **S.38:** a decree is executed by the court that passed it, or by a court to which it is transferred (S.39).
- **S.47:** **all questions** between the parties about execution are decided **by the executing court**. A separate suit is not allowed.
- **S.51, modes of execution:** delivery of property; attachment and sale; arrest and detention; appointment of a receiver.
- **S.58, civil prison:** decree above ₹5,000, up to **3 months**; decree between ₹2,000 and ₹5,000, up to **6 weeks**; decree up to ₹2,000, **no detention**.
- **S.60:** property exempt from attachment includes necessary clothing, tools of artisans, and the first ₹1,000 plus two-thirds of the rest of a salary.
- **O.21 R.97–101:** a third party who obstructs possession has their claim decided **within the execution** itself (*Silverline Forum v. Rajiv Trust*, 1998).
- **S.144, Restitution:** if a decree is reversed, the amount paid must be returned.
- **Limitation:** a decree can be executed within **12 years** (Art.136).

| Recent case | Point |
|---|---|
| *Rahul S. Shah v. Jinendra Kumar Gandhi* (2021, SC) | 14 directions to stop delays in execution. Execution must be completed within **6 months**, and the court should ascertain third-party interests at the stage of the suit itself. |
| *Periyammal v. V. Rajamani* (2025, SC) | The Court called the pendency of execution petitions alarming, directed High Courts to ensure they are disposed of within 6 months, and to monitor them. |

> **Trick:** "A decree without execution is like a cheque without a bank."
